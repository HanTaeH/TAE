## S1. Structured literature search (PubMed, run 2026-11-17)

Search 2 (broad; 773 records):

```
("Alzheimer Disease"[MeSH] OR alzheimer*[tiab]) AND (subtyp*[tiab] OR "molecular subgroup*"[tiab] OR stratif*[tiab] OR heterogeneity[tiab] OR cluster*[tiab] OR taxonomy[tiab]) AND (transcriptom*[tiab] OR "gene expression"[tiab] OR "RNA-seq"[tiab] OR microarray*[tiab] OR multi-omic*[tiab]) AND (brain[tiab] OR cortex[tiab] OR hippocamp*[tiab] OR postmortem[tiab] OR "post-mortem"[tiab] OR ROSMAP[tiab] OR MSBB[tiab] OR "Mayo"[tiab]) AND ("2015"[dp] : "2026"[dp])
```

Search 1 (narrow; 366 records, all contained in Search 2) was the same query without heterogeneity, cluster*, taxonomy and the cohort names.

Flow: 773 records; 139 excluded by publication type; 634 screened on title and abstract; 374 excluded by keyword rule; 260 reviewed manually (203 passing the rule plus 57 rule-excluded records whose titles mentioned subtypes or clusters); 226 excluded on review; 34 records from 30 studies included (27 de novo subtyping, 3 applications). Two further studies were not retrieved by the search and were added from other sources: Lai et al. 2022 (Front Immunol) and Wang et al. 2024 (Front Immunol 15:1462003). Total: 32 studies (Supplementary Table S2). Methods features of the 29 studies defining new subtypes were coded from the full text (at least the Methods) for 19 and from the abstract for 10; the 3 applied studies were coded separately (Section S8.6). The first author re-screened random samples without AI assistance (Section S5.4); agreement was 30/30 (κ = 1.00) in the second round, after a calibration round with κ = 0.39. This was a structured search reported with reference to the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Supplementary Fig. S2), not a full systematic review.

## S3. MSBB cross-region test against a CDR-shuffle null

Across the 17 MSBB regions, the region-level association of the C−A score with CDR was strongly related to that region's neuron-score association with CDR (ρ = −0.868, p = 6.4 × 10⁻⁶). To test whether this simply reflects the shared CDR vector, CDR was shuffled across subjects within region (500 permutations). The null median was −0.853 and the permutation p was 0.44, so the across-region relation is not evidence beyond what shared clinical labels produce. This test was therefore removed from the secondary family and is reported only here.

## S5. Supplementary Methods

### S5.1 SEA-AD donor pseudobulks
Besides the pseudobulk of all sequenced nuclei, we built:
1. Single-type pseudobulks: excitatory, inhibitory, astrocyte, oligodendrocyte and microglial nuclei, and the L2/3, L4, L5 and L6 IT subclasses. These remove differences between major types; the excitatory and inhibitory pseudobulks still mix their subclasses at each donor's own shares.
2. Excitatory, subclass mix fixed: each donor's own excitatory subclass profiles mixed at the cohort-average subclass shares, so that only within-subclass expression varies.
3. Fixed-composition pseudobulk: each donor's own type profiles mixed at the same proportions for every donor.
4. Composition-only pseudobulks: (a) cohort-average type profiles mixed at each donor's type proportions, with the total neuronal share fixed at 0.40 so that only the excitatory:inhibitory and glial ratios vary; (b) cohort-average excitatory subclass profiles mixed at each donor's subclass shares.

Laminar sampling: we related the share of each excitatory subclass among excitatory neurons, and the superficial-to-deep ratio (L2/3 IT + L4 IT versus L5/L6 types), to the C−A score, and computed mutual partial correlations of the L2/3 IT and L4 IT shares. We also adjusted the CPS and Braak associations for excitatory subclass composition: centred log-ratio coordinates of subclass nuclei counts (pseudocount 0.5 nuclei), entered on their own scale with age and sex. Result not shown in the main text: the superficial-to-deep ratio was unrelated to the score (ρ = −0.10), arguing against differences in dissection depth.

### S5.2 Simulation scenarios
Pseudobulk samples were built from the SEA-AD neurotypical reference with only the cell-type mixture varying, plus gene-level multiplicative noise and no disease-specific expression change: (i) fixed composition (exactly fixed, or with small jitter); (ii) neuron loss; (iii) glia-only change; (iv) heterogeneous change. To test whether neuron number can be distinguished from per-cell expression, we also simulated fixed composition with per-neuron expression lowered for (a) all neuronal mRNA, (b) only neuron-enriched genes, (c) neuron-enriched genes other than the marker genes. Lowering all neuronal mRNA with composition fixed is algebraically equivalent to lowering the neuronal share of RNA, so the classifier cannot distinguish the two by construction. The neuron-loss scenario was repeated over neuronal RNA content of 1–3 times that of glia and gene noise SD of 0.1–0.5.

### S5.3 Mediation models
We fitted forward (stage → composition → C−A score) and reverse (stage → C−A score → composition) mediation models on rank-standardised variables, with 2,000 bootstrap resamples. All 11 models are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Because the data are cross-sectional and the two scores are nearly collinear, these estimates are descriptive only.

### S5.4 Screening reliability
To assess screening reliability, the first author independently screened stratified random samples without AI assistance and blinded to the screening decisions. Calibration round (30 records): agreement 73%, Cohen's κ = 0.39; disagreements were discussed and the eligibility wording was clarified. Second round (a non-overlapping sample of 30 records): complete agreement (30/30; κ = 1.00; 95% CI for agreement 0.88–1.00). No screening decision was changed as a result of either round. An earlier check of 60 records (60/60 agreement) is not reported as a reliability estimate because the rater had used AI assistance for it.

### S5.5 RNA quality and deconvolution checks (detail)
In SEA-AD, RIN was related to pseudo-progression (ρ = −0.23) and to the neuronal fraction of nuclei (ρ = 0.36), and its association with the C−A score weakened after adjustment for both (ρ = −0.18, p = 0.11).

On SEA-AD donor pseudobulks with known proportions, NNLS estimated the major types well (Spearman ρ: excitatory neurons 0.82, astrocytes 0.96, oligodendrocytes 0.97, microglia 0.95, total neurons 0.94). This test is optimistic because the pseudobulks and the reference come from the same single-nucleus platform. Inhibitory subtypes were estimated poorly (ρ = 0.35–0.67; the residual inhibitory group was underestimated, 1.4% against a true 10.3%), so they were not interpreted.

## S6. Supplementary Results and Methods detail

### S6.1 Measurement-error correction of the neuron-adjusted stage association
Adjusting for a covariate measured with error leaves part of a confounded association behind. To estimate how much of the stage association that remained after neuron adjustment could be due to this, we estimated the reliability of the neuron score and of the C−A score from random split halves of their genes (Spearman–Brown, mean of 200 splits). We then recomputed the partial correlation with correlations disattenuated for both reliabilities (stage treated as error-free), with 500 bootstrap resamples per cohort, and pooled as for the primary endpoints. As a sensitivity analysis only half of the markers were used, to show the direction of the bias. Split-half reliability captures random error in the marker score, not how well the score measures neuronal content, and it assumes errors independent of the C−A score.

The disattenuated pooled partial correlation was 0.11 (95% CI 0.01–0.22; p = 0.04); with only half of the markers it was 0.08–0.20, showing the direction of the bias (Supplementary Table S11).

### S6.2 The neuron association in MSBB regions, with an oligodendrocyte covariate and in cerebellum
The association held in all 17 MSBB regions (ρ = −0.82 to −0.95; Supplementary Fig. S3) and after adjustment for an oligodendrocyte score (ρ = −0.86 to −0.92 in cortical cohort–region sets). The oligodendrocyte score had its own association with the C−A score given the neuron score (partial ρ 0.47–0.58 in four of six cortical sets, ≤ 0.12 in GSE33000 and GSE125583), so white-matter content adds to the axis but does not explain the neuron association. In cerebellum, where cortical markers fit poorly, the association was weaker (ρ = −0.51 with cortical markers, −0.30 with cerebellar markers); the oligodendrocyte score was the stronger correlate (partial ρ 0.63), and cortical and cerebellar neuron scores agreed poorly (ρ = 0.16).

### S6.3 RNA quality and robustness
RNA quality was associated with the C−A score in GSE106241 (lower RNA quality, higher scores: ρ = −0.37, p = 0.004; with lower neuron scores, ρ = 0.43) and in SEA-AD (lower RIN, ρ = −0.36, p = 0.001; lower pH, ρ = −0.27). Post-mortem interval and pH were weakly related to the score in MSBB (|ρ| ≤ 0.17). Effects on stage associations were mixed: MSBB CDR ρ = 0.31 → 0.32 after post-mortem interval and pH; GSE106241 Braak 0.24 (p = 0.066) before and after adjustment; SEA-AD pseudo-progression 0.25 → 0.19 (p = 0.09) after RIN, post-mortem interval and pH.

A region-matched dorsolateral prefrontal reference improved deconvolution fit in GSE33000 (R² 0.63–0.73 versus 0.53–0.65 with the temporal reference) without changing the conclusions. Results were also unchanged with five-subtype centroids, Spearman instead of Pearson projection, and random 80% subsets of classifier genes.

### S6.4 Composition adjustment and the residual axis
Within AD samples of each cortical cohort–region set, genes outside the classifier and marker lists were z-scored, residualised on the five marker scores and correlated with the composition-adjusted C−A score. Gene sets from MSigDB (Liberzon et al., 2015) GO Biological Process (3,718 sets with 15–500 measured genes) and Hallmark (50 sets) were scored by their mean gene correlation and tested competitively against all measured genes. The variance was inflated for inter-gene correlation, as in CAMERA (Wu and Smyth, 2012), with the inter-gene correlation estimated after removing the residual axis itself from each gene. We combined the cohorts by Stouffer's method (Stouffer et al., 1949) across four studies, averaging the three GSE118553 regions (same donors) first, and controlled the false discovery rate within each collection.

After adjustment, class assignments changed substantially (adjusted Rand index between original and adjusted labels 0.00–0.22 with marker scores and 0.09–0.24 with NNLS). A residual signal remained: mean best-class correlation was 59–66% of the unadjusted value after marker adjustment and 60–82% after NNLS adjustment, and it exceeded the random-gene and permuted-centroid nulls in every cohort. It was unrelated to post-mortem interval, pH, RNA quality, age or sex, and adjustment for 24 fine cell types barely reduced it (partial correlation 0.40 to 0.38 in GSE125583). Without clear discrete structure, the residual is best described as continuous variation.

No standard gene set was associated with the residual axis at FDR < 0.05 consistently in single cohorts. Combined across the four studies, however, 14 of 50 Hallmark sets and 377 of 3,718 GO sets were nominally enriched under this correlation-adjusted competitive test (FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Table S19). Nearly all of these had the same sign in all six cohort–region sets and on both platforms (13 of 14 Hallmark; 98% of GO sets). Genes lower on the class C side of the residual axis were enriched for protein secretion, axonal and microtubule-based transport, presynapse organisation and K48-linked ubiquitination (meta z −4.5 to −5.4), and genes higher on that side for cholesterol efflux, lipid and collagen metabolism and humoral immune response (z 3.0 to 3.8; GO results in the code repository). The residual axis therefore still points along the neuron–glia direction, reflecting neuronal and glial programmes that the five marker scores do not fully capture rather than a separate disease pathway. In earlier curated-list analyses, oxidative phosphorylation and ribosomal genes appeared to change sign between platforms, and long synaptic genes (for example GRIA2, NRXN1) lay at one end of the axis (Supplementary Note S4).

Removing the shared Banner donors from GSE132903 left the gene-set result unchanged (meta z correlation 0.99; 15 Hallmark and 338 GO sets). Genes lower on the class C side were enriched for Hallmark protein secretion (meta z = −5.4) and MYC targets (−3.4), and for GO axonal and microtubule-based transport, presynapse organisation, and K48-linked ubiquitination and deubiquitination (meta z −4.5 to −5.4); genes higher on that side for cholesterol efflux, lipid metabolism, collagen metabolism and humoral immune response (z 3.0 to 3.8). The 377 GO sets are listed in the code repository.

### S6.5 SEA-AD subclass composition and within-type associations
The score fell with the share of L2/3 IT neurons among excitatory neurons (ρ = −0.53) and rose with the L4 IT share (ρ = 0.59). These shares were strongly anti-correlated (ρ = −0.84), and only the L4 IT share remained associated given the other (partial ρ 0.32 versus −0.07). Adjusting for excitatory subclass composition removed the pseudo-progression association (0.25 → 0.03) and weakened the Braak association (0.39 → 0.27), but mixing cohort-average subclass profiles at each donor's shares gave an association of the opposite sign (ρ = −0.34). Subclass composition therefore marks donor progression without itself generating the axis.

Within cell types, the pattern was mixed:

- In neuronal pseudobulks, the score rose modestly with Braak stage (excitatory with subclass mix fixed ρ = 0.24; inhibitory 0.29; L4–L6 IT 0.27–0.34; age- and sex-adjusted), but not with pseudo-progression (excitatory 0.08) or dementia.
- Braak stage given pseudo-progression remained (ρ ≈ 0.24–0.30), whereas pseudo-progression given Braak did not (−0.11 to −0.20), although pseudo-progression is the finer, region-local measure.
- Within the 72 donors at Braak 4–6, the Braak association remained in inhibitory, L4 IT and L6 IT pseudobulks (ρ = 0.26–0.31) but not in excitatory pseudobulks (0.16).
- The L2/3 IT pseudobulk showed no Braak association (age- and sex-adjusted ρ = 0.10).
- In glial pseudobulks, the score was associated with dementia (astrocytes 0.26, oligodendrocytes 0.24, microglia 0.27) but not with Braak stage or pseudo-progression.

Across all 42 pseudobulk × outcome tests, 16 survived FDR < 0.05 on nominal p-values. Random-gene p-values were available for the 28 CPS and Braak tests, and 9 of these survived FDR < 0.05: the all-nuclei pseudobulk (CPS and Braak), the fixed-composition (Braak) and composition-only (CPS) pseudobulks, and the inhibitory, L4 IT, L5 IT and L6 IT pseudobulks (Braak; L6 IT also CPS) (Supplementary Table S15). The 12 donors at Braak 0–3 did not differ from the others in age, RIN, pH or neuronal fraction (all p > 0.1), but had longer post-mortem intervals (median 9.0 vs 6.5 h, p = 0.04).

### S6.6 Neff-style severity normalisation and neuronal SPV normalisation
Neff et al. first corrected MSBB expression for age at death, race, sex, post-mortem interval, batch and RIN with a mixed-effects model. They then considered two ways to remove the signal of neuronal loss: normalising by a neuronal surrogate proportion estimated with BRETIGEA (McKenzie et al., 2018), or by dementia severity. Both removed the correlation between cell-type proportions and CDR. Additional neuronal-SPV normalisation did not improve on CDR normalisation (their fig. S2C), and in ROSMAP they preferred MMSE normalisation because SPV normalisation left a residual association with severity. They therefore normalised the parahippocampal data by CDR before subtyping ("To remove the disease stage effect, CDR is corrected in the MSBB-AD gene expression data through linear model normalization"; ROSMAP was normalised for last known MMSE in the same way). Cell-type proportions were later reported per subtype rather than removed. To test the effect of the severity step, we residualised each gene on CDR, age, sex, post-mortem interval and pH within the AD samples of each MSBB region (22–35 AD subjects per region). We then projected the subtypes onto the residuals and recomputed the association between the C−A score and the neuron score. This approximates their procedure on a different platform. It is equivalent to a partial correlation given these covariates, so a near-zero association with CDR after normalisation is expected by construction; the test of interest is whether the neuron association survives.

We also applied the alternative that Neff et al. considered but did not use. In the AD samples of each MSBB region, a neuronal surrogate proportion variable (SPV) was computed as in BRETIGEA, as the first principal component of the scaled neuron marker genes not in the classifier (14 canonical markers on GPL96), with its sign set to agree with their mean. Neff et al. used BRETIGEA's larger marker set on RNA-seq, so this is an approximation. Each gene was residualised on the SPV, age, sex, post-mortem interval and pH (and, in a second variant, also on CDR), and the classes were projected again. We report the class proportions, agreement with the labels before normalisation (Cohen's κ), the correlation between the normalised and original C−A scores, and the associations with CDR and Braak stage, with the parahippocampal gyrus (the region Neff et al. subtyped) shown separately. To ask whether an SPV removes all neuronal signal, we built it from a random half of the markers and correlated the normalised C−A score with the other half's equally normalised score (median of 200 splits).

Neff et al. normalised expression for dementia severity and concluded that the "molecular signatures of these subtypes are independent from neuron degeneration and reactive gliosis seen in later stages of AD". The same paper reported, from BRETIGEA deconvolution, that "class C has a substantial loss of neurons accompanied by an increase of oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, OPCs, and endothelial cells, opposite of the changes observed in class A", and that the subtypes "differ strongly in neuronal activity despite normalization for AD staging", interpreting both as subtype biology. In AD samples of each MSBB region, our approximation of this normalisation removed the association between neuron score and CDR (median ρ −0.22 before, −0.01 after), as it must by construction. In the normalised data, however, the C−A score remained tightly correlated with the neuron score: median ρ = −0.91, range −0.88 to −0.96 across 17 regions (Fig. 3c). Severity normalisation removes only the part of neuronal variation that tracks severity; the subtypes capture the remaining between-person differences in neuronal signal.

Normalising instead by a neuronal surrogate proportion, the option Neff et al. considered, changed the subtypes themselves. Before normalisation the SPV explained a median 81% of the variance of the C−A score (range 68–89%; 86% in the parahippocampal gyrus). After normalisation the classifier still assigned all three classes in similar proportions (median A 37%, B 36%, C 28%), as random gene sets also do, but many patients changed class: agreement with the original labels was moderate (median κ = 0.48, range 0.18–0.73; parahippocampal gyrus 0.43). The normalised C−A score correlated with the original at a median ρ of 0.40, as expected when the SPV explains about four-fifths of its variance (√0.19 ≈ 0.44). It was not associated with CDR (median ρ 0.00 against 0.18 before; parahippocampal gyrus 0.14 against 0.53) and only weakly with Braak stage (median 0.12). When the SPV was built from about seven markers, the normalised score still correlated with the remaining markers (median ρ = −0.38, range −0.56 to −0.25), so a surrogate built from few genes leaves residual neuronal signal; whether a larger marker set would do so could not be tested on this platform. Adding CDR to the normalisation gave the same results (κ = 0.39; Supplementary Table S9). With SPV normalisation, with or without CDR, about four-fifths of the variance of the C−A score was removed and a substantial fraction of patients was regrouped. CDR normalisation, by contrast, left the neuronal axis intact.

### S6.7 Simulation details
When proportions were exactly fixed, the C−A score was unrelated to the marker neuron score (ρ = 0.07). Neuronal fraction jitter with an SD of only 1.1 percentage points already produced ρ = −0.60, showing how closely the axis is aligned with the neuronal direction in gene space. Results were stable across neuronal RNA content of 1–3 times that of glia and gene noise SD of 0.1–0.5 (ρ = −0.92 to −0.99). Bulk data therefore cannot tell fewer neurons from lower expression per neuron, and we refer to the axis as neuronal signal.

## S7. Data processing and additional results

### S7.1 Data processing
GSE84422 probes were mapped with the GPL96 annotation; probes mapping to several genes were removed, and for each gene the probe with the highest mean expression was kept. GSE132903 was designed balanced for age and sex between AD and controls. The 62 donors shared by GSE132903 and GSE125583 were matched on case identifiers in the sample titles. In GSE106241 the RNA-quality variable is the ordinal 1–4 grade supplied with the series, derived from RIN measured by Bioanalyzer; we assume a higher grade is better, which fits its positive correlation with the neuron score. The reference-free single-sample score ranks genes within each sample, scales ranks to ±0.5 (singscore approach) and takes the mean scaled rank of genes up in a class minus that of genes down in it. Neff et al. defined AD clinically and excluded mixed pathology, whereas MSBB AD here follows CERAD category.

### S7.2 Class assignment
In the four MSBB regions profiled by Neff et al., class proportions were A 34–48%, B 16–34% and C 31–41%. Simulated samples with fixed composition gave 32% A, 31% B and 37% C, and the SEA-AD all-nuclei pseudobulk gave 49% A, 32% B and 19% C.

### S7.3 Marker overlap and composition
With the 48 strictly non-specific classifier genes (±0.5 threshold), the C−A score correlated with the neuron score at ρ = −0.64 to −0.87. When neuronal markers were split at random, adjusting for one half removed 74–88% of the association with the other (500 splits). Composition explained more of the variance of individual classifier genes than of random genes in five of six cortical sets but not in GSE132903 (47% vs 45%, p = 0.07).

### S7.4 Case–control separation against random genes, unadjusted
The unadjusted values gave the same picture as the age- and sex-adjusted ones (GSE33000 0.64 vs a random median of 0.59, p = 0.037, an excess of only 0.05). Without the shared Banner donors the adjusted pooled estimate was 0.42 (0.33–0.50) or 0.41 (0.28–0.52), depending on the cohort from which they were removed. GSE118553 case–control separation was 0.42 in temporal, 0.33 in entorhinal and 0.14 in frontal cortex. With frontal cortex the pooled prediction interval was −0.15 to 0.74; the lower frontal value fits the later involvement of frontal cortex (same donors, so descriptive only).

### S7.5 Simulation and histology detail
Lowering all neuronal mRNA gave classes of 26%, 32% and 43%; lowering only neuron-enriched genes gave ρ = 0.90 with the extent of lowering; for neuron-enriched genes other than the markers, the C−A score tracked the marker neuron score at ρ = +0.24. In SEA-AD, the all-nuclei association with NeuN density held given pseudo-progression (−0.26, 95% CI −0.47 to −0.03; −0.16 without the four low-NeuN donors) and was absent for the composition-only pseudobulk (−0.13); the NeuN+ share of nuclei fell with pseudo-progression and Braak stage (−0.29 and −0.27).

## S8. Further nulls, positive control, subtype schemes, CSF projection, residual axis and literature coding

### S8.1 Strength-matched null (post hoc)
The specificity-matched null pooled all non-neuronal types into one class, and classifier glia-enriched genes were more specific than the pool (median |log₂ neuron/glia ratio| 3.9 vs 2.0). After the classifier exceeded that null in GSE33000 (p = 0.004), the excess persisted in 30 subsamples of 52, 97 and 219 AD samples (median p 0.015–0.017) and with a finer match on the top non-neuronal type (p = 0.01). We then matched genes on the log₂ ratio in 1-unit bins within expression tertile (500 sets per cohort). Observed ρ and strength-matched null medians: GSE33000 −0.93 vs −0.91, GSE132903 −0.92 vs −0.88, GSE118553 −0.85 vs −0.81, GSE125583 −0.93 vs −0.94. P for the classifier being more coupled: 0.06, 0.08, 0.24, 0.89; less coupled: 0.94, 0.93, 0.76, 0.11. Against the specificity-matched draws, P less coupled: 1.00, 0.86, 0.60, 0.23. (An earlier run of the strength-matched null with other random draws gave 0.054, 0.08, 0.23 and 0.91 for the more-coupled tail.) Code: gse33000_null_followup.py, bound_upper_tail.py; Supplementary Table S5.

### S8.2 κ against random covariates and further subtype schemes
In MSBB, κ between labels before and after residualising every gene on one covariate was 0.85 for CDR, a median 0.79 (5th percentile 0.52) for 200 single random genes and 0.48 for the neuronal SPV (medians of 17 regions); the SPV κ was lower than 95% of random covariates in 8 regions (median p 0.06; Supplementary Table S8; msbb_normalisation_kappa_null.py). Further schemes: Lai et al. (2022; CXCR4, PPP3R1, HSP90AB1, CXCL10, S100A12) and Ma et al. (2023; 15 m7G-related genes) were applied to AD samples of the four independent cohorts. The PC1 of the z-scored genes was the subtype axis; k-means (k = 2) labels agreed with consensus hierarchical labels at ARI 0.29–0.87, so the continuous axis is the main measure (Supplementary Table S17; other_schemes_clustered.py). The PANoptosis scheme of Wang et al. (2024) was also run in the code, but its 24-gene list came from a secondary summary, did not contain the neuronal core genes the authors describe and conflicts with the 14 genes in their Fig. 4C; it is not reported until the list is verified.

### S8.3 Positive-control simulation
Cohorts of 100 controls (neuronal loss 0–15%) and 200 AD cases (10–60%) were built from SEA-AD cell-type profiles with i.i.d. gene-level noise. A composition-independent signal along the published C−A contrast of the 257 classifier genes present in the reference was added at 0–100% of its size, as a within-AD subtype score or as a case–control difference. A within-AD subtype signal makes the classifier less neuron-coupled than random genes, so that test uses the upper tail; the neuron-adjusted case–control test uses the upper tail of the partial correlation. Detection rate (share of 20 cohorts with p < 0.05 against 200 specificity- or strength-matched random sets), within AD, 200 AD cases: 0/0% at 0%, 0/0% at 2%, 10/35% at 5%, 100/100% at ≥ 10%. Re-run at the four real AD sizes (52, 97, 219, 310), specificity/strength: at 0%, 0% throughout; at 5%, 0–10%/20–40%; at 10%, 60/75% (n = 52), 95/100% (97), 95/100% (219), 100/100% (310); at 25%, 100% throughout. With 20 replicates, 0/20 has an upper 95% limit of 17% and 20/20 a lower limit of 83%. Case–control (200 AD): 50/20% at 0%, 95/95% at 2%, 100% at ≥ 5%, so that test is anti-conservative. At f = 0.1 the observed ρ fell from about −0.85 to −0.76, i.e. the added component is about a fifth of the C−A score variance in AD. Limits: the same reference defines and generates specificity (oracle matching), the noise has no correlated non-composition component, and the signal lies along the projection's own contrast. Code: positive_control_simulation.py; Supplementary Table S6.

### S8.4 CSF proteomic subtypes (supplementary analysis)
Proteins of the five CSF subtypes of Tijms et al. (2024; 1 hyperplasticity, 2 innate immune activation, 3 RNA dysregulation, 4 choroid plexus dysfunction, 5 blood–brain barrier dysfunction; their Supplementary Table 4) were mapped to genes (1,058 → 875–951 per cohort; SEA-AD class 441 glia-enriched, 469 non-specific, 133 neuron-enriched, 15 absent) and scored as a signed mean z-score in AD samples. All five tracked the neuron score (|ρ| 0.80–0.94) and the Neff C−A score (|ρ| 0.77–0.95) in every cohort; subtypes 1–3 scored high where neuronal signal was high, 4–5 where it was low. The neuron association exceeded expression-matched nulls in 16/20 subtype–cohort pairs, specificity-matched in 7/20 and strength-matched in 6/20 (all in GSE118553 and GSE125583). After removing Neff genes (823–916 genes left) and adjusting both scores for five cell scores, Tijms–Neff residual coupling was |ρ| 0.27–0.67 with consistent signs; it exceeded strength-matched nulls in 7/20 pairs: subtype 5 in GSE132903 (p 0.007), GSE118553 (0.023) and GSE125583 (0.003); subtype 2 in GSE118553 (0.023) and GSE125583 (0.003); subtype 4 in GSE125583 (0.003); subtype 3 in GSE132903 (0.040). CSF levels index release and clearance, not tissue stock, so the projection shows that the proteins separating the subtypes have different cellular origins in brain tissue, not that patients differ in neuronal content. The residual coupling of subtype 5 may reflect mural or perivascular-fibroblast composition that one endothelial marker score does not capture.

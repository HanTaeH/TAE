## Abstract

**Background:** Transcriptomic subtypes of Alzheimer's disease (AD) from bulk post-mortem brain are proposed for target discovery and stratification. Bulk expression depends on cell composition, but no subtype-defining study we identified compared its subtypes with random genes matched on cell type. We built this benchmark and applied it to published schemes and de novo subtyping, focusing on Neff et al. (2021), whose subtypes were described as independent of the neuronal degeneration seen in later stages of AD.

**Methods:** Four independent public cohorts (1,066 samples) and a 17-region cohort from the Neff brain bank were projected onto the published genes or centroids of Neff et al., Li et al. (2022), Lai et al. (2022) and Ma et al. (2023), and AD samples were clustered de novo on their most variable genes. Axes were compared with a neuronal marker score and with random genes matched on cell-type specificity. For the Neff classifier we randomised its cell-type directions, reproduced both normalisations the authors considered and added a composition-independent signal to measure power.

**Results:** The Neff C−A score tracked the neuron score (pooled ρ = −0.92), as did two of three Li subgroups (|ρ| 0.61–0.96) and principal axes of the Lai and Ma genes in six of eight pairs. De novo subtypes followed the neuron score in three cohorts (η² median 0.59) and vascular–microglial scores in the fourth. Random genes with the Neff classifier's cell-type mix reproduced its coupling, which came from the direction it assigns to each cell-type class; the Li subgroups were more coupled than such genes, and the Neff C1−C2 split did not follow composition. The benchmark had 80% power for a composition-independent component of 5% of score variance in RNA-seq but only 21–52% in arrays. Case–control separation (ρ = 0.43) fell to 0.14 after neuron adjustment; Braak and tangle associations fell to near zero.

**Conclusions:** The main axis of the Neff subtypes, of several published gene-set schemes and of de novo subtypes in bulk AD cortex is largely cell composition, mostly neuronal signal. Subtypes should be benchmarked against cell-type-matched random genes, with a spike-in power check, before they are read as distinct mechanisms.

## Background

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is clinically and pathologically heterogeneous (Murray et al., 2011), and molecular subtyping has been proposed as a route from that heterogeneity to mechanism-based stratification. In a structured search we identified 29 studies since 2015 that defined new transcriptomic subtypes from bulk post-mortem brain ({T:lit}; Supplementary Note S1). They clustered samples on co-expression networks (Neff et al., 2021), hub genes (Li et al., 2022), immune signatures (Lai et al., 2022) or pathway gene sets (Ma et al., 2023), described the subtypes by the pathways that separate them, such as immune activation, synaptic signalling or metabolism, and nominated their marker genes as targets. Proteomics of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) has added subtypes defined in living patients (Tijms et al., 2024).

That bulk brain expression is shaped by cell composition is well established. Neuronal and glial programmes account for much of the variation between samples (Srinivasan et al., 2016; Kelley et al., 2018), co-expression modules and case–control signals in AD cohorts largely track cell-type proportions (McKenzie et al., 2017; Mostafavi et al., 2018; Wan et al., 2020), and composition shifts with pathology as neurons are lost and glia respond (Patrick et al., 2020). The subtyping literature has addressed this only in part. None of the 19 studies whose methods we coded from full text compared its subtypes with random genes matched on cell type, and none of the 29 reported doing so; 13 reported cell-type differences between subtypes as a subtype feature, 8 of them with immune-infiltration tools only, and 2 used composition estimates for adjustment (Supplementary Note S8.6). Eteleeb et al. (2024) included deconvolution estimates among the features of their AMP-AD subtypes and found one subtype marked by neuronal loss, and Al-Ouran et al. (2026) showed that a latent factor in about 2,500 brain RNA-seq samples reflects where the tissue was sampled. Neff et al. (2021) addressed neuronal loss most directly, by normalisation. Whether a subtype axis carries information beyond the composition of the sample, and whether the analysis would detect such information if it were there, has not been tested against a composition-matched benchmark.

The subtypes of Neff et al. (2021) are the case in which this question was addressed most directly. Derived from RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of the parahippocampal gyrus and three other regions in the Mount Sinai Brain Bank (MSBB), they divide AD cases into three classes and five subtypes, were reproduced by the authors in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of 391 AD cases from the Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP); the 271 classifier genes were published with their class-mean expression, although the random forest itself was not. Class C, concordant with a hippocampal AD signature (Blalock et al., 2004) and called typical, was described as amyloid-predominant and immune-activated; class A as atypical with up-regulated synaptic and neuronal pathways. Neff et al. reported fewer neurons and more glia in class C by deconvolution (McKenzie et al., 2018) and, noting that neuronal loss is "both the cause and effect of molecular changes leading to cognitive impairment", normalised expression by dementia severity before subtyping. They concluded that "both normalization by CDR and cell-type proportion SPVs effectively remove the confounding effects of neuronal loss along disease progression" and described the subtype signatures as "independent from neuron degeneration and reactive gliosis seen in later stages of AD", while suggesting that "subpopulations of neuronal or glial cell types may contribute distinctively to different AD subtypes".

Here we built such a benchmark (study design in Fig. 1a; Box 1). We applied it to four published schemes whose genes or centroids can be measured in public cohorts and to de novo clustering with the field's usual recipe, comparing each subtype axis with random gene sets matched on expression and cell-type specificity. Because the Neff centroids allow the original contrast to be reproduced, we examined that classifier in depth: we asked where its coupling with neuronal signal comes from, added a composition-independent signal to the real data to show what the comparison can detect, and related the axis to the two normalisations the original study considered, to disease stage and local tangle burden, and to single-nucleus data.

**Box 1. What the benchmark asks.** Random genes that have the same cell-type make-up as a subtype's genes carry the composition of the sample and nothing else. If a subtype carried information beyond composition, its axis would be less tightly tied to neuronal signal than such random genes. Adding a known composition-independent signal to the real data shows how large that information must be before the comparison sees it. The benchmark therefore detects information that is orthogonal to cell-type programmes; biology that moves along those programmes, such as selective neuronal vulnerability, is indistinguishable from composition by design.

## Methods

### Data and cohorts

All data were public and de-identified (Table 1). Four independent bulk cohorts from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) had AD and control samples: GSE33000 (Narayanan et al., 2014), GSE132903 (Piras et al., 2019), GSE118553 (Patel et al., 2019) and GSE125583 (Srinivasan et al., 2020). GSE106241 (Marttinen et al., 2019) was used only for the Braak stage association. GSE84422 (Wang et al., 2016), an Affymetrix array cohort of 17 regions from the MSBB, comes from the brain bank of the original study, may share donors with it and was treated as a same-brain-bank comparison, not an independent replication. The Seattle Alzheimer's Disease Brain Cell Atlas (SEA-AD; Gabitto et al., 2024) provided a single-nucleus reference from neurotypical donors, donor pseudobulks and quantitative neuropathology for 84 donors. Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) v8 cortex was used to trace a residual axis (GTEx Consortium, 2020). GSE132903 and GSE125583 share 62 donors (sampled from different regions); every pooled estimate reported in the text was repeated with the shared donors removed from either cohort ({T:shared}; Supplementary Note S2). The MSBB RNA-seq data used to define the subtypes and ROSMAP are under controlled access and were not analysed.

### Projection of published subtypes

The published centroids are mean expression of the 271 random-forest genes per class in MSBB AD samples, "after CDR normalization and cohort effect correction" (Neff et al., Data file S2); the contrast between classes is therefore already a property of severity-normalised data. We centred them per gene and projected each sample by Pearson correlation with each centroid after z-scoring genes across the AD samples of the cohort (across all samples for the stage endpoint), assigning the class with the highest correlation. The main axis was summarised as the C−A score, r(C) − r(A), and class B as r(B) − mean[r(A), r(C)]; the subtype contrasts C1−C2 and B1−B2 used the five subtype centroids. The random forest itself was not applied. Five-subtype centroids, Spearman projection, random 80% gene subsets and a reference-free single-sample score (Foroutan et al., 2018) served as sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Note S5).

### Cell-type specificity and composition

Each classifier gene was labelled with the SEA-AD neurotypical middle temporal gyrus reference as neuron-enriched if log₂[(highest neuronal type + 1)/(highest non-neuronal type + 1)] exceeded 1, glia-enriched if below −1, and non-specific otherwise. Marker scores were the mean z-score of canonical markers for neurons (16 genes, 13–15 of them measured per cohort; 14 on GPL96) and for astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes and endothelial cells, excluding classifier genes; the neuron score was also split into excitatory, inhibitory and pan-neuronal synaptic components. We use the neuron score as a measure of the neuronal contribution to a sample's RNA, which may reflect neuron number, per-neuron expression, dissection and RNA quality. Reference-based deconvolution used non-negative least squares (NNLS) against SEA-AD profiles, validated on the 84 donor pseudobulks. To check circularity, the neuronal markers were split at random into halves (500 splits), one half measuring the association and the other adjusting for it.

### Random-gene nulls

To ask whether a result reflected the classifier genes rather than any gene set, each analysis was repeated with random genes of the same number that inherited the centroid row of the classifier gene they replaced (Venet et al., 2011), excluding classifier and marker genes (Table 2). The specificity-matched null gives random sets the classifier's cell-type mix; the strength-matched null was added after the classifier exceeded the specificity-matched null in GSE33000, because its glia-enriched genes are more specific than the pool. In no cohort was a stratum empty or smaller than needed. Empirical p-values were (k + 1)/(N + 1). Because random genes inherit the classifier's centroid rows, these nulls keep the direction the classifier gives each cell-type class. Two further nulls asked whether that direction is what carries the coupling: the classifier's own genes with centroid rows permuted within specificity class (keeping the class-level direction) or multiplied by random signs (removing it), compared with a classifier in which every gene carries the mean centroid row of its class.

### Other published subtype schemes

The selection rule was set after the Neff analysis and is stated here as applied. A scheme could be tested if the paper gave the genes or centroids that define its subtypes and these could be measured in public array and RNA-seq cohorts. Seventeen of the 29 subtype-defining studies, mostly multi-omic, whole-transcriptome or large-gene-set schemes, many on controlled-access AMP-AD data (for example Milind et al., 2020; Zheng and Xu, 2021; Iturria-Medina et al., 2022; Eteleeb et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2026), did not meet it; the recipe they share was tested instead by de novo clustering (below). Of the others, we tested Neff et al. (2021), whose centroids allow its contrast to be reproduced, and three gene-set schemes, Li et al. (2022), Lai et al. (2022) and Ma et al. (2023). Eight further small gene-set schemes (2–48 genes) qualified but were not tested, apart from one reported only in the Supplementary Note, because the first principal component of random gene sets of that size already tracks neuronal signal (below), so a comparison would carry little information (status per study in {T:lit}). For the hub-gene subgroups of Li et al. (2022; ten genes per subgroup from their Table 1), each subgroup score was the mean z-score of its genes. Lai et al. (2022) defined immune-microenvironment subtypes from infiltration scores and then selected five diagnostic signature genes; Ma et al. (2023) clustered patients on 15 m7G-related genes. For these two the axis was approximated by the first principal component of the z-scored genes, because k-means labels agreed only moderately with consensus clustering (adjusted Rand index 0.29–0.87; Supplementary Note S8.2); for Lai this is a proxy for the diagnostic signature, not for the original subtypes. Each axis was related to the neuron score (scheme genes removed from the markers) and compared, in both tails, with random sets of the same size matched on expression decile and on expression decile and specificity class. As a supplementary analysis, the proteins of the five CSF subtypes of Tijms et al. (2024; their Supplementary Table 4) were mapped to genes, the five subtype means were centred per gene across subtypes, and each brain sample was scored by its Pearson correlation with each centred profile, as for the Neff centroids (Supplementary Note S8.4). A PANoptosis scheme (Wang et al., 2024) is reported only in Supplementary Note S8.2, because the gene list available to us could not be verified against the paper.

### De novo subtyping

To test the field's usual recipe without any published gene list, the AD samples of each independent cohort were clustered de novo ({T:denovo}). Genes in the top 60% of mean expression were ranked by median absolute deviation, and the 1,500 most variable, excluding all marker genes, were z-scored. Samples were clustered by consensus non-negative matrix factorisation (genes shifted to a minimum of zero; 50 resamples of 80% of samples; average-linkage clustering of the consensus matrix) and by k-means on the first ten principal components, for k = 2, 3 and 4; the first principal components served as continuous axes. For each labelling we recorded the share of neuron-score variance between subtypes (η²) and the cross-validated balanced accuracy with which the five marker scores predicted the labels (multinomial logistic regression, five-fold cross-validation repeated five times), compared with 100 sets of five random-gene scores of the same sizes and expression deciles drawn from genes outside the clustered 1,500. The analysis was repeated after regressing the five marker scores out of every clustered gene, with agreement to the unadjusted labels measured by the adjusted Rand index (ARI; Supplementary Note S8.10).

### Spike-in positive control, detection and equivalence

In the AD samples of each independent cohort we added a composition-independent subtype signal to the observed expression of the classifier genes, X[g, j] + f × s_j × (C − A)_g, with s_j drawn from a standard normal distribution and (C − A) the published centroid contrast, for f from 0 to 0.5 (200 replicates, each with a bootstrap resample of samples). Random genes were not spiked, so the real nulls are unchanged. For each f we recorded the shift in neuron coupling (Δρ), the share of C−A variance explained by the added component, and the detection rate, the share of replicates in which the planned upper-tail test against the matched null gave p < 0.05. Power is reported as the variance share at which the detection rate reached 50% and 80%, interpolated on the grid. As a secondary summary, assuming that the classifier has no offset from the null, a one-sided 95% upper confidence limit was obtained by test inversion with the combined variance of the null over gene sets and of the coupling over bootstrap resamples; when the observed coupling already exceeded the null at f = 0, no limit is defined. The classifier's offset from each matched null was pooled across cohorts by random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian–Laird between-cohort variance, Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman 90% CI) and tested for equivalence with margins of ±0.05 and ±0.03 (two one-sided tests). The margins were chosen after the offsets were seen; on the spike-in scale an offset of 0.03 corresponds to roughly 4–6% of C−A variance (Supplementary Note S8.3).

### Normalisation, cross-region agreement, stage and neuropathology

In the AD samples of each MSBB region we approximated the CDR normalisation by residualising each gene on CDR, age, sex, post-mortem interval and pH. For the alternative, cell-type normalisation, we re-implemented BRETIGEA (McKenzie et al., 2018) in our reading of Neff et al.'s fig. S2C (their main text refers to neuronal SPVs; we used all six types, and a single neuronal SPV as a variant): surrogate proportion variables (SPVs) for neurons, astrocytes, endothelial cells, microglia, oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursors from up to 1,000 human brain markers per type (500 for precursors; 203–616 present on GPL96), each gene residualised on all six SPVs with the same covariates. The same was done in the AD samples of the four GEO cohorts with age and sex. Labels before and after were compared with Cohen's κ against six principal components of random 1,000-gene sets used in the same way (200 draws), and the share of C−A variance explained by the six SPVs was compared with that explained by the six random components; a single neuronal SPV from the 14 markers served as a variant ({T:kappa}). In MSBB, agreement of the scores across the regions of each subject was measured by the intraclass correlation (ICC(1)) after standardising scores within region, for the C−A score, the neuron score and the C−A score adjusted for the neuron score, against random gene sets matched on expression or specificity.

Case–control separation was the age- and sex-adjusted rank correlation of the C−A score with diagnosis (control < AD; GSE118553 also with asymptomatic AD, analysed separately as a sensitivity). Within-cohort analyses used Braak stage in GSE125583 and GSE106241, and CDR, Braak stage, the sum of regional tangle (neurofibrillary tangle, NFT) densities, the CERAD sum and neuritic plaque density in MSBB (subject means of cortical regions), across the 106 subjects with cortical samples, in the 64 CERAD-defined AD subjects and in the 58 of them with CDR ≥ 1 (the cross-region analysis also excluded the hippocampus, required two or more regions and took the diagnostic group from a subject's first listed sample, giving 105 subjects and 65 AD). Associations were repeated after adjustment for the neuron score. If neuronal loss lies on the path from pathology to the score, this adjustment removes stage associations by construction; if sample quality affects both scores, the neuron score is also a collider and the adjusted association is not interpretable as a direct effect. We therefore report forward and reverse partial correlations side by side, and partial correlations with semipartial correlations on the scale of the unadjusted score.

### Single-nucleus data

For the 84 SEA-AD donors, pseudobulks were built from all nuclei, from single broad cell types (composition fixed by construction) and with composition held fixed (Supplementary Note S5.1), and related to the continuous pseudo-progression score (CPS), Braak stage and dementia, to NeuN, GFAP and Iba1 histology of the same region, to co-pathology (LATE-NC stage, Lewy body disease, APOE ε4), and, for the key regulators Neff et al. nominated, to within-type expression, against a C−A score recomputed without the tested gene and without the tested type's nuclei. False discovery rate (FDR) control was applied within each block.

### Further analyses

The genome-wide subtype signatures of Neff et al. (Data file S1) were split into a part predicted from SEA-AD cell-type expression and a residual (Supplementary Note S8.9). A composition-adjusted residual axis was traced in MSBB, GTEx and SEA-AD (Supplementary Note S8.5).

### Statistics

Two endpoints were fixed after initial exploratory analyses: (1) the association of the C−A score with the neuron score in AD samples, and (2) case–control separation. Each was pooled across the four independent cohorts by random-effects meta-analysis of Fisher-transformed correlations (bootstrap standard errors, restricted maximum likelihood between-cohort variance, Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman intervals, 95% prediction intervals; I² from Cochran's Q). With four cohorts these intervals, and prediction intervals in particular, are imprecise. Endpoint 2 was first defined as an unadjusted mix of the four cohorts and GSE106241 and redefined as age- and sex-adjusted in the four independent cohorts before the neuron-adjusted analyses; both are reported ({T:primary}). Endpoint 1 against zero is descriptive; the inference rests on the comparison with cell-type-matched nulls, which was placed in the secondary family with the per-cohort claims. All other p-values from every result file (2,377) formed the exploratory family; Benjamini–Hochberg FDR was controlled within each family and globally ({T:tests}). Empirical p-values near 0.05 are reported with their Monte Carlo standard error. The analysis plan was not preregistered. Analyses used Python 3 with fixed random seeds; code and a script that reproduces every result are at [repository URL and archive DOI; to be made public before submission].

### Literature search and use of a language model

A structured PubMed search (17 November 2026; Supplementary Note S1; Supplementary Fig. S2) identified 32 studies that defined or applied transcriptomic AD subtypes from bulk post-mortem brain; 29 defined new subtypes, and their methods were coded from the full or partial text (19) or the abstract (10) ({T:lit}). A large language model (Claude, Anthropic; [model version and dates of use to be completed]) was used under the authors' direction to write and run analysis code, assist screening (checked by the first author), draft and edit text and run simulated internal reviews. ## Results

### Published schemes and de novo subtypes mostly follow the neuronal signal, but not uniformly

The main axes of the Neff classifier, two of the three Li subgroups and de novo subtypes in three of four cohorts followed the neuron score; the exceptions are reported alongside (Fig. 1b).

*Published schemes.* The Neff C−A score was negatively correlated with the neuron score in every cohort (ρ −0.85 to −0.93). Of the Li et al. (2022) hub-gene subgroups, subgroup II tracked the neuron score in every cohort (ρ 0.94–0.96) and subgroup I inversely (−0.61 to −0.86), whereas subgroup III did not consistently (|ρ| 0.07–0.78). Against random genes of the same specificity mix the Li subgroups were more neuron-coupled in 7 of 12 subgroup–cohort pairs (subgroup II in all four, p ≤ 0.002; subgroup I in three) and less coupled in none ({T:schemes}); the subgroup II hub genes, such as SYN2 and ATP6V1C1, are synaptic genes that fall below our two-fold threshold for neuronal enrichment. The first principal components of the five Lai et al. (2022) signature genes and the 15 Ma et al. (2023) genes tracked the neuron score in six of eight scheme–cohort pairs (|ρ| 0.60–0.84) but not Lai in GSE118553 (0.23, against a matched-null median of 0.43) or Ma in GSE125583 (0.31). None of these eight pairs differed from specificity-matched random genes (p 0.10–0.79). Random sets of the same size gave principal components that themselves tracked the neuron score (median |ρ| 0.32–0.79), so for small gene sets this comparison has little power, and the Lai and Ma results show mainly that the largest axis of most small gene selections in AD cortex is neuronal.

*De novo subtypes.* Clustering the 1,500 most variable genes of AD samples, with no published gene list and all marker genes removed, gave stable subtypes (cophenetic correlation 0.85–0.99) whose main axis was cell composition in every cohort ({T:denovo}). In GSE132903, GSE118553 and GSE125583 the first principal component tracked the neuron score (|ρ| 0.87, 0.69 and 0.97), and across the 18 labellings (k = 2–4, NMF and k-means) the neuron score differed strongly between subtypes (η² 0.22–0.74, median 0.59); the five cell scores accounted for 31–98% of the between-subtype variance of the clustered genes. GSE33000 was the exception: its subtypes did not differ in neuron score (η² ≤ 0.05), and its first principal component instead followed endothelial and microglial scores (ρ −0.65 and −0.62; cell scores explained 64% of its variance). After the five cell scores were regressed out of every gene, clustering produced different subtypes (median ARI with the original labels 0.15, range −0.003 to 0.64). Five random-gene scores drawn outside the clustered genes predicted the labels about as well as the cell scores (better than random only for k = 2 in GSE125583, p = 0.01), as expected if the subtypes follow the dominant axis of the tissue, which most genes share.

*CSF subtypes (supplementary).* Proteins of the five CSF subtypes of Tijms et al. (2024), projected onto brain tissue, also tracked the neuron score (|ρ| 0.80–0.94 in all 20 subtype–cohort pairs, which share genes and are far from independent). They were more coupled than specificity-matched random genes in 7 pairs (GSE118553 and GSE125583) and less coupled in some others, such as subtype 1 in GSE33000 (0.80 against 0.91) and GSE125583 (0.87 against 0.94) ({T:csf}; Supplementary Note S8.4). Because CSF levels reflect release and clearance, this shows only that the proteins separating the CSF subtypes come from different cell types in brain, not that the patients differ in neuronal content.

The remaining sections examine the Neff subtyping, whose published centroids allow its contrast and its claim of independence from neuronal loss to be tested directly.

### Classes can be assigned in every cohort, but balanced classes are expected by design

Between 80% and 96% of the classifier genes were measured in each cohort. All three classes were present in every cohort and region, in proportions close to the discovery cohort (A 28–46%, B 15–32%, C 31–48%; Fig. 2a), and, consistent with Neff et al., class C had the lowest neuron score. Random gene sets carrying the same centroid rows also gave all three classes (median 34–47% A), because expression is z-scored within each cohort and each sample is assigned to its nearest centroid ({T:exprnull}). The classifier nevertheless assigned somewhat more samples to class C than random genes in three cohort–region sets. Class labels therefore describe a sample's position within its cohort.

### The classifier is built along a neuron–glia axis

Of the 153 classifier genes higher in class C than A and present in the reference, 119 were glia-enriched; 40 of the 44 neuron-enriched genes were higher in class A (Fig. 2c). Neuronal specificity was anti-correlated with the C−A direction of the centroid across genes (ρ = −0.83). This agrees with the original random forest, in which lower expression of neuronal genes such as SCAMP5, SV2B and KCNB1 best predicted class C (Neff et al., fig. S11B).

### The C−A score tracks neuronal signal as cell-type-matched random genes do

In AD samples the C−A score was negatively correlated with the neuron score in every cohort (ρ = −0.85 to −0.93; pooled −0.92, 95% CI −0.94 to −0.90; prediction interval −0.95 to −0.89; Fig. 3a,b). The association was robust to removing the shared donors, to glial covariates, to split markers, to region and to reference-free scoring (−0.77 to −0.95; {T:cohortv11}; Supplementary Note S6), and was carried mainly by pan-neuronal synaptic markers.

Random genes matched on expression alone were far less coupled (median ρ −0.09 to −0.73; p ≤ 0.016; Fig. 2b), but random genes with the classifier's cell-type mix reproduced the coupling (median −0.84 to −0.94; {T:specnull}). In three cohorts the classifier did not differ from them in either direction (lower-tail p 0.14–0.78; upper-tail p 0.23–0.86). In GSE33000 the classifier was more coupled than the specificity-matched null (Δρ = −0.05, p = 0.004) and marginally more than the strength-matched null (Δρ = −0.02, p = 0.066 ± 0.004 Monte Carlo SE; {T:strength}). This excess is in the direction of more, not less, composition dependence; because a pure-composition classifier is expected to sit slightly beyond the null (offset about 0.02 in simulation; {T:poscon}), we read it as a calibration check rather than as evidence for or against subtype signal. The offsets differed between cohorts (Cochran's Q p = 0.018), so they were pooled by random effects. The pooled offset was −0.021 (90% CI −0.060 to 0.017) for specificity matching and −0.009 (−0.039 to 0.021) for strength matching ({T:spikev12}). With strength matching the offset was equivalent to zero within ±0.05 but not within ±0.03; with specificity matching equivalence could not be shown at either margin.

The coupling came from the direction the classifier assigns to each cell-type class ({T:direction}). Giving every classifier gene the mean centroid row of its specificity class reproduced the coupling (ρ −0.77 to −0.96; correlation with the observed score 0.93–0.95), whereas random signs on the same rows removed it (median ρ −0.03 to 0.03). Gene-level information within classes made the classifier slightly more neuron-coupled in the three array cohorts (Δρ −0.02 to −0.08; within-class permutation p ≤ 0.024) and slightly less in GSE125583 (Δρ +0.03, p = 0.002).

Whether the comparison can detect information beyond composition was tested by adding a composition-independent signal to the real data (Fig. 3d; {T:spike}). A spiked component explaining 10% of C−A variance moved the coupling by a similar amount in every cohort (Δρ ≈ 0.04–0.06), but the upper-tail test against the specificity-matched null detected such a component reliably only in the RNA-seq cohort. The test reached 80% power at about 5% of C−A variance in GSE125583, at 21% in GSE33000, 29% in GSE132903 and 52% in GSE118553 (52 AD cases); 50% power was reached at 3%, 16%, 23% and 34% ({T:spikev12}). The benchmark can therefore exclude a composition-independent component of about 5% of the score in one cohort and only components of a fifth to a half of the score in the array cohorts. Assuming no offset from the null, the one-sided 95% upper limits were 3% (GSE132903), 6% (GSE125583) and 21% (GSE118553) with specificity matching (0.5–14% with strength matching); in GSE33000 the classifier was already more coupled than the specificity-matched null, so no limit is defined.

### Severity normalisation leaves the neuron axis; cell-type normalisation removes it

In AD samples of each MSBB region, our approximation of the CDR normalisation removed the association between the neuron score and CDR (median ρ −0.22 before, −0.01 after), as it must by construction, but left the C−A score coupled to the neuron score (median ρ −0.91, range −0.88 to −0.96; Fig. 3c). This agrees with Neff et al., who reported fewer neurons in class C in MSBB (their Fig. 4A) and, after MMSE normalisation, in ROSMAP (their Fig. 5D), and with the provenance of the published centroids, whose contrast was derived after CDR normalisation.

The alternative that Neff et al. considered, normalisation on BRETIGEA cell-type SPVs, removed the neuron coupling but also showed how unstable the class labels are ({T:bretigea}). The six SPVs explained 67–94% of C−A variance in MSBB regions (median 86%) and 82–92% in the GEO cohorts. Six principal components of random 1,000-gene sets already explained a median 76% in MSBB and 50–84% in the GEO cohorts; the SPVs explained more in 9 of 17 MSBB regions and in three of four GEO cohorts. After normalisation the C−A score no longer tracked the neuron score (median ρ 0.01) and kept little of its original ranking (ρ with the original 0.04–0.30), and most patients changed class (median κ 0.15 in MSBB; 0.10–0.26 in the GEO cohorts). Six random components used in the same way also changed most labels (median κ 0.29 in MSBB; 0.19–0.53), fewer than the SPVs in three of four GEO cohorts but in only 6 of 17 MSBB regions, where about 30 AD subjects per region were adjusted for ten covariates. In the parahippocampal gyrus, the discovery region, the SPVs changed fewer labels than random components (κ 0.36 against 0.29), the CDR association of the score fell (0.55 to 0.16) and its tangle association rose (0.31 to 0.43). A single neuronal SPV from 14 markers, closer to the wording of Neff et al.'s main text, changed labels about as much as random 14-gene components explaining a somewhat smaller share of the score (median R² 0.71 against 0.61; κ 0.48 against 0.50; {T:kappa}; {T:spv}). Cell-type normalisation therefore removes the neuronal part of the main axis, but class labels are unstable under any adjustment that removes the leading axes of the data, so label change alone does not show what the classes are made of.

### Case–control separation and stage associations follow the neuronal signal

The C−A score separated AD from controls (age- and sex-adjusted pooled ρ = 0.43, 95% CI 0.34–0.51; I² = 0; Fig. 4c; {T:primary}), as did the neuron score (pooled ρ −0.45). Random genes matched on expression gave a similar separation in two of four cohorts and those matched on specificity in three ({T:specnull}). After adjustment for the neuron score the partial correlation was 0.14 (0.04–0.23), and 0.06–0.24 depending on how neuronal signal was modelled ({T:stageextra}; {T:neuronadj}); the reverse partial, of the neuron score given the C−A score, was −0.15 (−0.25 to −0.04). On the scale of the unadjusted score (semipartial) the remainder was 0.02–0.08 (pooled 0.05), because the adjusted score retains only 19–25% of its variance ({T:cohortv11}). Removing the shared donors changed these estimates by at most 0.03 ({T:shared}). The remainder was not larger than with expression-matched random genes in any cohort ({T:adjnull}); it exceeded specificity-matched random genes only in GSE125583 (p = 0.002), but this neuron-adjusted comparison had a false-positive rate of 20–50% in simulation and is uninformative in that direction ({T:poscon}).

Within AD, the C−A score rose with Braak stage in GSE125583 (217 cases with Braak stage; age- and sex-adjusted ρ = 0.26, 95% CI 0.12–0.39; Fig. 4a) and in MSBB (64 subjects, 0.32, 0.08–0.54; 58 with CDR ≥ 1, 0.33, 0.09–0.56), but not with CDR (0.18, −0.09 to 0.42; Fig. 4b), in agreement with Neff et al., whose Kruskal–Wallis tests across subtypes were not significant for CDR in all cases (p = 0.082) or for CDR and Braak stage in the 55 cases with regional pathology (p = 0.155 and 0.075). Across the 106 MSBB subjects the C−A score rose with the sum of regional tangle densities (0.26, 0.08–0.44), which the neuron score tracked (−0.27, −0.45 to −0.08), but not with neuritic plaque density (0.11, −0.08 to 0.29) or the CERAD sum (0.09); within AD the plaque association was 0.08 (−0.17 to 0.30) ({T:nft}). This does not reproduce the higher plaque density that Neff et al. reported in class C, which they called amyloid-predominant; our plaque measure is a subject-level average on a related array cohort, not their regional ratings. After adjustment for the neuron score, the Braak and tangle associations were near zero (for example GSE125583 Braak 0.00, −0.14 to 0.14; MSBB tangle density 0.02, −0.17 to 0.21; {T:stagefu}; {T:nft}), although the intervals do not exclude small remainders; the CDR association in AD with CDR ≥ 1 became negative (−0.22, p = 0.10). Forward and reverse mediation models gave overlapping estimates ({T:mediation}).

### The axis is consistent within a person because neuronal signal is

In MSBB, a subject's C−A scores agreed across regions (ICC 0.61, 95% CI 0.54–0.68; 73% of a subject's regions carried the subject's most frequent class, against 53% with subjects shuffled), as did the neuron score (0.66) ({T:crossregion}). Random genes with the classifier's cell-type mix were as consistent (ICC 0.60, p = 0.33), whereas expression-matched genes were not (0.30, p = 0.002). Subject means of the C−A and neuron scores correlated at −0.96. After adjustment for the neuron score, the score kept a weaker subject-level component (ICC 0.35 against 0.30 for specificity-matched genes, p = 0.10) that was unrelated to CDR, Braak stage, tangle density, post-mortem interval or pH (|ρ| ≤ 0.13).

### Neuron number, per-cell expression and glial state

Bulk data cannot separate fewer neurons from less RNA per neuron: lowering all neuronal mRNA is algebraically the same as lowering the neuronal share, and in pseudobulk simulations neuron loss alone reproduced all three classes and a C−A score coupled to neuronal fraction (ρ = −0.98; Fig. 5b; Supplementary Note S6.7).

In 84 SEA-AD donors (exploratory), the all-nuclei C−A score rose with pseudo-progression (ρ = 0.25), Braak stage (0.39) and dementia (0.38) (Fig. 5a; {T:seaadpb}). Within single cell types, where composition is fixed, the score rose modestly with Braak stage in inhibitory pseudobulks (0.29) and, not surviving FDR control, in excitatory ones (0.24), and lower NeuN density went with a class C shift in astrocyte, oligodendrocyte and microglial pseudobulks (ρ −0.28 to −0.29 in all donors, −0.24 to −0.28 after excluding four donors with near-absent NeuN staining, a threshold set after inspecting the data) but not in neuronal ones ({T:histo}). GFAP and Iba1 histology were unrelated to the all-nuclei score; the one nominal association was a higher inhibitory-pseudobulk score with GFAP area (ρ = 0.29, p = 0.008; {T:histo}). The axis therefore includes coupled changes of cell state inside neuronal and glial types, as well as composition. Within excitatory and inhibitory nuclei, Neff et al.'s neuronal regulators ATP6V1A (ρ −0.58 and −0.53) and NAPB (−0.33) were lower in donors with higher C−A scores, as reported for class C, whereas SYT1 was higher (0.60 and 0.37), opposite to its reported direction; these associations held with the C−A score recomputed without the tested gene and the tested type's nuclei ({T:regloo}; {T:regulators}). Co-pathology was not associated with the score after FDR control.

### Class B and the C subtypes

The Neff class B score fell with the oligodendrocyte score in three cohorts (ρ −0.50 to −0.58), more than matched random genes in two and less in two ({T:cohortv11}), and the B1−B2 contrast tracked the neuron score (0.57–0.78) as closely as or less than matched random genes. The C1−C2 contrast was the clearest exception. Its coupling with the neuron score (0.34, 0.41 and 0.52 in the array cohorts, −0.04 in GSE125583) differed in all four cohorts from what the classifier's cell-type directions predict: permuting centroid rows within specificity class gave strongly negative coupling (null medians −0.46 to −0.75; p ≤ 0.01), so the gene-level weights within each cell-type class turn the C1−C2 split away from neuronal signal. Against specificity-matched random genes it was less negatively coupled in three cohorts (medians −0.09 to −0.71; p 0.008–0.03) and less positively coupled in GSE33000 (median 0.51; p = 0.008) ({T:direction}). The split of class C is therefore the part of the Neff subtyping least explained by composition, and our conclusions concern the main axis, not class B or the C subtypes.

### What remains after composition adjustment

Cell scores explained 82–89% of the variance of the C−A score ({T:residual}). A continuous composition-adjusted residual axis replicated across cohorts, was also present in GTEx cortex of donors without dementia and resembled the gene-wise signature of RNA integrity (r = 0.43–0.50; gene-set tests in {T:hallmark}); its origin is uncertain (Supplementary Note S8.5). Splitting the genome-wide signatures of Neff et al. gave the same picture: cell-type expression predicted 54% of the C−A signature across genes, but the remainder still produced sample scores coupled to the neuron score (ρ −0.82 to −0.90), and after removing cell scores it was associated with diagnosis only in GSE125583 and not with Braak stage within AD ({T:signature}; Supplementary Note S8.9).

## Discussion

The main axes of the Neff classifier, of two of three Li subgroups, of the Lai and Ma gene sets in most cohorts and of de novo subtypes in three of four cohorts followed the neuronal signal of the sample; in the fourth cohort de novo subtypes followed vascular and microglial signal instead. For the Neff classifier, random genes with its cell-type mix reproduced the coupling, which came from the direction it gives each cell-type class. The Li subgroups were more neuron-coupled than such genes, which is more, not less, composition dependence, and the split of Neff class C was the one contrast that composition did not explain. Two observations explain why composition recurs. Within AD in bulk cortex, the largest axis of variation of most gene selections, and of the whole transcriptome in most cohorts, is cell composition, so clustering samples is likely to split them along it. The genes that separate the resulting clusters are then described by their pathways, and synaptic, immune and metabolic pathways are themselves cell-type programmes; in the Neff classifier most genes that mark class C are glia-enriched and nearly all neuron-enriched genes mark class A, as the original random forest also showed. That bulk expression depends on composition is known, and Eteleeb et al. (2024) and Al-Ouran et al. (2026) found neuronal loss and sampling location among the factors of large AMP-AD cohorts. What our results add is a benchmark that asks whether a subtype axis carries anything beyond composition, with a spike-in that states how much it could have detected. Its power was good in one RNA-seq cohort and modest in arrays, so the absence of a composition-independent signal is established only for components of about 5% or more of the Neff score in RNA-seq, and of a fifth or more in arrays.

### Relation to the original study

For the Neff subtyping our results speak to one claim rather than to the subtypes as a whole. Neff et al. reported the neuronal difference between classes themselves, limited their claim of independence to neuronal change that tracks severity ("seen in later stages of AD"), and anticipated that neuronal or glial subpopulations could contribute to the subtypes. Our data agree that severity normalisation removes the part of neuronal variation that tracks dementia severity, but differences in neuronal signal between patients at similar severity remain, and the main axis captures them. Neff et al. kept severity normalisation for two reasons: in MSBB, adding SPV normalisation did not further reduce the remaining correlation (their fig. S2C), and in ROSMAP, SPV normalisation left residual correlation with cognition. In our reproduction, cell-type normalisation reassigned most patients, but so did six random components explaining a similar share of the score, and in the parahippocampal gyrus it reassigned fewer than random components did; the class structure is therefore not specifically what cell-type normalisation removes. The ROSMAP replication they reported is expected under our reading, since a neuron–glia axis is present in any cortical bulk cohort. Whether such differences are a confound or a meaningful dimension of AD, such as selective vulnerability, resilience or co-pathology, our data do not settle; they show that the axis cannot be read as independent of them.

Some results point the other way and are open. In GSE125583, the one RNA-seq cohort and the only one with power to detect a component of about 5%, the classifier was slightly less coupled than matched genes and kept a neuron-adjusted case–control remainder; class B was less coupled than matched genes in two cohorts; and the C1−C2 contrast did not follow the classifier's cell-type directions. In the parahippocampal gyrus, the discovery region, the tangle association of the C−A score rose after cell-type normalisation (ρ 0.31 to 0.43) while the CDR association fell (0.55 to 0.16). The original labels, classifier and RNA-seq data were not available to us, and our cohorts were mostly prefrontal and temporal arrays rather than parahippocampal RNA-seq, so the size of these effects in the original setting may differ.

### What the axis measures

In bulk tissue the neuron score has several sources: neuronal loss, loss of synaptic transcripts per neuron, which precedes neuron loss in AD (DeKosky and Scheff, 1990; Terry et al., 1991), dissection of grey and white matter, and pre-analytical factors that act preferentially on neuronal transcripts (Li et al., 2004). Our data do not separate them; the score is dominated by synaptic genes, and the SEA-AD data point to cell state as well as number. The axis is better described as the local neurodegeneration signal of the sampled tissue than as a distinct process. It tracked tangle density more closely than plaque density, as neuronal loss does (Gómez-Isla et al., 1997). This fits the regional tangle differences Neff et al. reported in a subset of 55 cases, but not their description of class C as amyloid-predominant and of classes A and B as tau-predominant.

Reading the axis as local neurodegeneration does not make it uninteresting. The best-validated AD subtypes are defined by where tangles and neuronal loss fall: hippocampal-sparing, typical and limbic-predominant AD (Murray et al., 2011), and atrophy and tau-PET subtypes. A class C–class A split in parahippocampal tissue could be the transcriptomic trace of limbic-predominant against hippocampal-sparing disease. Testing this needs regional tangle counts, which the public MSBB deposit gives only as a sum across regions, or imaging of the same patients. What our data show is that such a split would be read from neuronal content, not from a separate molecular programme.

### Stage

Neuron adjustment reduced stage associations to small remainders. If tangles cause neuronal loss, which lowers the neuron score, adjustment removes such associations whatever the classifier carries, and the reverse model fitted equally well; if sample quality affects both scores, adjustment can also induce associations. The data therefore show neither that the subtypes are stages of one process nor that they are independent of stage.

### Implications

Subtype names drawn from pathway enrichment, such as immune-active, synaptic or metabolic, should be read against the cell-type programmes those pathways belong to. A key regulator that is neuron- or glia-enriched is not thereby a poor target. ATP6V1A and NAPB were lower within neuronal nuclei in donors further along the axis, which supports a per-cell component worth testing, while SYT1 moved opposite to its reported direction. Regulators nominated from bulk subtypes should therefore be checked within cell types before follow-up. Post-mortem subtypes stratify patients only through living proxies, which should be checked against regional atrophy of the sampled area.

### Recommendations

We suggest that transcriptomic subtype studies of bulk brain tissue (1) compare subtype signal with random gene sets matched on expression and on cell-type specificity, projected identically and in both directions, and test whether the signal survives randomising its cell-type directions; (2) add a composition-independent signal to the real data and report the power of that comparison; (3) report subtype scores as continuous variables with and without adjustment for composition estimated by two methods; and (4) report RNA quality, dissection (for example an oligodendrocyte score), local neuropathology and co-pathology alongside the scores.

### Limitations

The subtypes were approximated by centroid projection, mostly on arrays and in other regions than the discovery tissue, and the original RNA-seq data, labels and classifier were not analysed; the decisive test would relate bulk RNA-seq to same-tissue cell counts, for example in ROSMAP (Patrick et al., 2020). The other schemes were approximated by subtype scores or first principal components of their published genes rather than by their original clustering, for Lai et al. by their diagnostic signature rather than their subtypes, and the CSF subtypes by brain RNA of their proteins. The schemes were selected by a rule stated after the Neff analysis; the large AMP-AD schemes could not be reproduced and were represented only by de novo clustering on public cohorts, whose random-gene comparison has little power because most genes share the dominant axis. Regional tangle densities, needed to relate the axis to neuropathological subtypes (Murray et al., 2011), are not in the public MSBB deposit. Power was estimated for a composition-independent signal lying along the published contrast and added to the real data, with the null computed on the original rather than the resampled samples; the upper limits also assume that the classifier has no offset from the null, and the equivalence margins were chosen after the offsets were seen. Cell-type-matched nulls cannot exclude biology that is aligned with cell-type programmes. BRETIGEA markers were incompletely measured on GPL96, and MSBB regions had few AD subjects for ten covariates. RNA quality was not available for the GEO cohorts. Co-pathology was available only in SEA-AD; LATE-NC, Lewy body and vascular pathology were not excluded in the GEO cohorts, unlike in the discovery cohort. AD definitions differed between cohorts, MSBB AD was defined by CERAD category and CDR was assessed retrospectively. NeuN density is a two-dimensional density, not neuron number, and came from a different block than the sorted nuclei. Cortical markers and a temporal-cortex reference were applied to all regions. Endpoints were fixed after exploratory analyses, the strength-matched null was post hoc, and 10 of 29 studies were coded from abstracts.

## Conclusions

In bulk AD cortex, the main axis of the Neff et al. (2021) subtypes, of several published gene-set schemes and of de novo subtypes is largely cell composition, mostly neuronal signal. For the Neff classifier, random genes with the same cell-type mix recover this axis, through the direction the classifier assigns to each cell-type class; the split of class C is the exception. A composition-independent component could be excluded down to about 5% of score variance only in RNA-seq. Subtypes of bulk brain tissue should be tested against cell-type-matched random gene sets, with a spike-in that reports the power of the comparison, before they are interpreted as distinct mechanisms or used for target nomination and stratification.

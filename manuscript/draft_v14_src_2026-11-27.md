# Cell composition, mostly neuronal signal, forms the main axis of Alzheimer's disease transcriptomic subtypes in bulk brain: a benchmark with cell-type-matched random genes

## Abstract

**Background:** Transcriptomic subtypes of Alzheimer's disease (AD) from bulk post-mortem brain are proposed for target discovery and stratification. Bulk expression depends on cell composition, but no subtype-defining study we identified compared its subtypes with random genes matched on cell type. We built this benchmark and applied it to published schemes and de novo subtyping, focusing on Neff et al. (2021), whose subtypes were described as independent of the neuronal degeneration seen in later stages of AD.

**Methods:** Four independent public cohorts (1,066 samples) and a 17-region cohort from the Neff brain bank were projected onto the published genes or centroids of Neff et al., Li et al. (2022), Lai et al. (2022) and Ma et al. (2023), and AD samples were clustered de novo on their most variable genes. Axes were compared with a neuronal marker score and with random genes matched on cell-type specificity. For the Neff classifier we randomised its cell-type directions, reproduced both normalisations the authors considered and added a composition-independent signal to measure power.

**Results:** The Neff C−A score tracked the neuron score (pooled ρ = −0.92), as did two of three Li subgroups (|ρ| 0.61–0.96) and principal axes of the Lai and Ma genes in six of eight pairs. De novo subtypes followed the neuron score in three cohorts (η² median 0.57) and vascular–microglial scores in the fourth. Random genes with the Neff classifier's cell-type mix reproduced its coupling, which came from the direction it assigns to each cell-type class; the Li subgroups were more coupled than such genes, and the Neff C1−C2 split did not follow composition. The benchmark had 80% power for a composition-independent component of 5% of score variance in RNA-seq but only 21–52% in arrays. Case–control separation (ρ = 0.43) fell to 0.14 after neuron adjustment; Braak and tangle associations fell to near zero.

**Conclusions:** The main axis of the Neff subtypes, of several published gene-set schemes and of de novo subtypes in bulk AD cortex is largely cell composition, mostly neuronal signal. Subtypes should be benchmarked against cell-type-matched random genes, with a spike-in power check, before they are read as distinct mechanisms.

## Background

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is clinically and pathologically heterogeneous (Murray et al., 2011), and molecular subtyping has been proposed as a route to mechanism-based stratification. In a structured search we identified 29 studies since 2015 that defined transcriptomic subtypes from bulk post-mortem brain ({T:lit}; Supplementary Note S1). They clustered samples on co-expression networks (Neff et al., 2021), hub genes (Li et al., 2022), immune signatures (Lai et al., 2022) or pathway gene sets (Ma et al., 2023), named the subtypes after the pathways that separate them and nominated their marker genes as targets. Proteomics of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) has added subtypes in living patients (Tijms et al., 2024).

Bulk brain expression is shaped by cell composition. Neuronal and glial programmes account for much of the variation between samples (Srinivasan et al., 2016; Kelley et al., 2018), co-expression modules and case–control signals in AD track cell-type proportions (McKenzie et al., 2017; Mostafavi et al., 2018; Wan et al., 2020), and composition shifts as neurons are lost and glia respond (Patrick et al., 2020). The subtyping literature has addressed this only in part. None of the 19 studies whose methods we coded from full text compared its subtypes with random genes matched on cell type, and none of the 29 reported doing so; 13 described cell-type differences between subtypes as a subtype feature (Supplementary Note S8.6). Eteleeb et al. (2024) used deconvolution estimates as subtype features and found a subtype marked by neuronal loss, and Al-Ouran et al. (2026) found a latent factor in about 2,500 brain RNA-seq samples that reflects where tissue was sampled. Whether a subtype axis carries information beyond composition, and whether an analysis could detect it, has not been tested against a composition-matched benchmark.

Neff et al. (2021) addressed neuronal loss most directly. Their subtypes, derived from RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of the parahippocampal gyrus and three other regions in the Mount Sinai Brain Bank (MSBB), divide AD cases into three classes and five subtypes and were reproduced in 391 cases from the Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP); the 271 classifier genes were published with their class means, the random forest was not. Class C, called typical, was described as amyloid-predominant and immune-activated, and class A as atypical with up-regulated synaptic pathways. Neff et al. reported fewer neurons in class C, normalised expression by dementia severity before subtyping, and described the signatures as "independent from neuron degeneration and reactive gliosis seen in later stages of AD".

Here we built a composition-matched benchmark (Fig. 1a; Box 1) and applied it to four published schemes whose genes or centroids can be measured in public cohorts and to de novo clustering. Because the Neff centroids allow the original contrast to be reproduced, we examined that classifier in depth: where its neuronal coupling comes from, what the benchmark can detect, how the two normalisations the authors considered act on it, and how it relates to stage, tangles and single-nucleus data.

**Box 1. What the benchmark asks.** Random genes with the same cell-type make-up as a subtype's genes carry the composition of the sample and nothing else. A subtype that carried information beyond composition would be less tightly tied to neuronal signal than such genes. Adding a known composition-independent signal to the real data shows how large that information must be to be seen. The benchmark therefore detects information orthogonal to cell-type programmes; biology that moves along those programmes, such as selective neuronal vulnerability, is indistinguishable from composition by design.

## Methods

### Data and cohorts

All data were public and de-identified (Table 1). Four independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) cohorts had AD and control samples: GSE33000 (Narayanan et al., 2014), GSE132903 (Piras et al., 2019), GSE118553 (Patel et al., 2019) and GSE125583 (Srinivasan et al., 2020); GSE106241 (Marttinen et al., 2019) was used only for Braak stage. GSE84422 (Wang et al., 2016), a 17-region MSBB array cohort, comes from the original brain bank, may share donors with it and was treated as a same-brain-bank comparison. SEA-AD (Gabitto et al., 2024) provided a single-nucleus reference, donor pseudobulks and quantitative neuropathology for 84 donors, and GTEx v8 cortex served to trace a residual axis (GTEx Consortium, 2020). GSE132903 and GSE125583 share 62 donors; every pooled estimate was repeated without them ({T:shared}; Supplementary Note S2). The MSBB RNA-seq and ROSMAP data are under controlled access and were not analysed.

### Projection and marker scores

The published centroids are class means of the 271 random-forest genes in MSBB AD samples "after CDR normalization and cohort effect correction" (Neff et al., Data file S2), so the class contrast is already severity-normalised. We centred them per gene and projected each sample by Pearson correlation with each centroid after z-scoring genes across the AD samples of the cohort, assigning the closest class. The main axis was the C−A score, r(C) − r(A); class B was r(B) − mean[r(A), r(C)], and the C1−C2 and B1−B2 contrasts used the five subtype centroids. Sensitivity analyses are in Supplementary Note S5.

Classifier genes were labelled neuron-enriched, glia-enriched or non-specific with the SEA-AD neurotypical reference (log₂ ratio of the highest neuronal to the highest non-neuronal type above 1 or below −1). Marker scores were mean z-scores of canonical markers for neurons (16 genes, 13–15 measured per cohort) and for astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes and endothelial cells, excluding classifier genes. The neuron score measures the neuronal contribution to a sample's RNA, which may reflect neuron number, per-neuron expression, dissection and RNA quality.

### Random-gene nulls

Each analysis was repeated with random genes of the same number that inherited the centroid row of the classifier gene they replaced (Venet et al., 2011), excluding classifier and marker genes (Table 2). The specificity-matched null gives random sets the classifier's cell-type mix; the strength-matched null was added post hoc because the classifier's glia-enriched genes are more specific than the pool. Empirical p-values were (k + 1)/(N + 1). For the neuron association, the lower tail asks whether the classifier is more neuron-coupled than random genes, and the upper tail whether it is less coupled, as a composition-independent signal would make it. Two direction nulls permuted the classifier's centroid rows within specificity class or gave them random signs.

### Other schemes and de novo subtyping

The selection rule was stated after the Neff analysis: a scheme was testable if its paper gave the genes or centroids that define the subtypes and these could be measured in public cohorts. Seventeen of the 29 studies, mostly multi-omic or whole-transcriptome schemes on controlled-access AMP-AD data (for example Milind et al., 2020; Zheng and Xu, 2021; Iturria-Medina et al., 2022; Eteleeb et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2026), did not qualify; their shared recipe was tested by de novo clustering instead. Besides Neff, we tested Li et al. (2022; mean z-score of ten hub genes per subgroup), Lai et al. (2022; five diagnostic signature genes) and Ma et al. (2023; 15 m7G-related genes), the last two summarised by the first principal component of their genes. Eight further small gene-set schemes qualified but were not tested, because principal components of random gene sets of that size already track neuronal signal (status per study in {T:lit}; Supplementary Note S8.2). Axes were compared with the neuron score and with random sets matched on expression and specificity, in both tails.

For de novo subtyping, the AD samples of each cohort were clustered on the 1,500 genes with the largest median absolute deviation among the top 60% by expression, excluding marker genes ({T:denovo}; Supplementary Note S8.10). We used consensus non-negative matrix factorisation (NMF; 50 resamples of 80% of samples) and k-means on ten principal components, for k = 2–4. Per labelling we recorded the share of neuron-score variance between subtypes (η²) and the cross-validated accuracy with which five marker scores predicted the labels, against 100 sets of random-gene scores drawn outside the clustered genes, before and after regressing the marker scores out of every gene (adjusted Rand index, ARI). As a supplementary analysis, CSF subtype proteins (Tijms et al., 2024) were mapped to genes and projected like the Neff centroids (Supplementary Note S8.4).

### Spike-in positive control and equivalence

In AD samples of each cohort we added a composition-independent signal along the published C−A contrast to the classifier genes, X[g, j] + f × s_j × (C − A)_g with s_j standard normal, for f from 0 to 0.5 (200 bootstrap replicates); random genes were not spiked. Power was the share of replicates in which the planned upper-tail test against the matched null gave p < 0.05, reported as the share of C−A variance at which it reached 50% and 80%. Upper confidence limits by test inversion, which assume no offset from the null, are secondary. The classifier's offset from each null was pooled by random effects (DerSimonian–Laird, Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman 90% CI) and tested for equivalence at ±0.05 and ±0.03, margins chosen after the offsets were seen (Supplementary Note S8.3).

### Normalisation, cross-region agreement, stage and single-nucleus data

In AD samples of each MSBB region we approximated CDR normalisation by residualising genes on CDR, age, sex, post-mortem interval and pH. For cell-type normalisation we re-implemented BRETIGEA (McKenzie et al., 2018) in our reading of Neff et al.'s fig. S2C: six surrogate proportion variables (SPVs) from up to 1,000 markers per type, regressed out with the same covariates (age and sex in the GEO cohorts). Labels before and after (Cohen's κ) and the share of C−A variance explained were compared with six principal components of random 1,000-gene sets; a single neuronal SPV served as a variant ({T:kappa}). Agreement across regions of a subject was measured by ICC(1).

Case–control separation was the age- and sex-adjusted rank correlation of the C−A score with diagnosis. Within AD we used Braak stage (GSE125583, GSE106241) and, in MSBB subject means of cortical regions, CDR, Braak stage, summed tangle (NFT) density, CERAD sum and neuritic plaque density (106 subjects, 64 AD, 58 with CDR ≥ 1). Associations were repeated after adjustment for the neuron score; because neuronal loss may lie on the path from pathology to the score, and sample quality may affect both, forward and reverse partial correlations are reported side by side. SEA-AD pseudobulks of all nuclei and of single cell types were related to pseudo-progression, Braak stage, dementia, histology, co-pathology and the key regulators of Neff et al. (Supplementary Note S5.1). The genome-wide signatures of Neff et al. and a composition-adjusted residual axis were analysed as in Supplementary Notes S8.5 and S8.9.

### Statistics, literature and language model

Two endpoints were fixed after initial exploration: the C−A–neuron association in AD samples and case–control separation, each pooled across the four cohorts by random-effects meta-analysis of Fisher-transformed correlations (REML, Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman intervals, 95% prediction intervals) ({T:primary}). The comparisons with cell-type-matched nulls formed the secondary family and all other p-values (2,377) the exploratory family; Benjamini–Hochberg FDR was controlled within families ({T:tests}). The plan was not preregistered. ## Results

### Published schemes and de novo subtypes mostly follow neuronal signal, but not uniformly

*Published schemes* (Fig. 1b). The Neff C−A score was negatively correlated with the neuron score in every cohort (ρ −0.85 to −0.93). Li subgroup II tracked it in every cohort (ρ 0.94–0.96) and subgroup I inversely (−0.61 to −0.86), but subgroup III did not consistently (|ρ| 0.07–0.78). The Li subgroups were more neuron-coupled than specificity-matched random genes in 7 of 12 subgroup–cohort pairs and less coupled in none ({T:schemes}); the subgroup II hub genes, such as SYN2 and ATP6V1C1, are synaptic genes below our threshold for neuronal enrichment. The principal axes of the Lai and Ma genes tracked the neuron score in six of eight pairs (|ρ| 0.60–0.84; not Lai in GSE118553, 0.23, or Ma in GSE125583, 0.31) and never differed from specificity-matched genes (p 0.10–0.79). Principal axes of random sets of the same size also tracked the neuron score (median |ρ| 0.32–0.79), so these small sets mainly show that the largest axis of most gene selections in AD cortex is neuronal.

*De novo subtypes.* Clustering the 1,500 most variable genes gave stable subtypes (cophenetic correlation 0.85–0.99) whose main axis was composition in every cohort ({T:denovo}). In GSE132903, GSE118553 and GSE125583 the first principal component tracked the neuron score (|ρ| 0.87, 0.69, 0.97), subtypes differed strongly in neuron score (η² 0.22–0.74, median 0.57 over 18 labellings) and the five cell scores accounted for 31–98% of between-subtype variance. In GSE33000 subtypes did not differ in neuron score (η² ≤ 0.05); the first component followed endothelial and microglial scores (ρ −0.65 and −0.62). Regressing out the cell scores gave different subtypes (median ARI 0.15). Random-gene scores predicted the labels about as well as cell scores (better only for k = 2 in GSE125583, p = 0.01), as expected when subtypes follow the dominant axis that most genes share.

*CSF subtypes (supplementary).* Projected onto brain, the five CSF subtypes also tracked the neuron score (|ρ| 0.80–0.94 in 20 non-independent pairs), more than specificity-matched genes in 7 pairs and less in some, such as subtype 1 in GSE33000 (0.80 against 0.91) ({T:csf}). This shows that the proteins separating the CSF subtypes come from different brain cell types, not that the patients differ in neuronal content.

### The Neff classifier is built along a neuron–glia axis

Between 80% and 96% of classifier genes were measured per cohort, and all three classes appeared in every cohort and region (A 28–46%, B 15–32%, C 31–48%; Fig. 2a). Random genes carrying the same centroid rows also gave all three classes (median 34–47% A; {T:exprnull}), because each sample is assigned to its nearest centroid within its cohort. Of the 153 classifier genes higher in class C than A, 119 were glia-enriched, and 40 of the 44 neuron-enriched genes were higher in class A (Fig. 2c); neuronal specificity was anti-correlated with the centroid contrast across genes (ρ = −0.83), as in the original random forest (Neff et al., fig. S11B).

### The C−A score tracks neuronal signal as cell-type-matched random genes do

In AD samples the pooled C−A–neuron correlation was −0.92 (95% CI −0.94 to −0.90; prediction interval −0.95 to −0.89; Fig. 3a,b), robust to shared donors, glial covariates, split markers, region and reference-free scoring (−0.77 to −0.95; {T:cohortv11}; Supplementary Note S6). Expression-matched random genes were far less coupled (median ρ −0.09 to −0.73; Fig. 2b), but specificity-matched genes reproduced the coupling (median −0.84 to −0.94; {T:specnull}). In three cohorts the classifier did not differ from them in either tail (p 0.14–0.86). In GSE33000 it was more coupled than the specificity-matched null (Δρ = −0.05, p = 0.004) and marginally more than the strength-matched null (Δρ = −0.02, p = 0.066; {T:strength}); a pure-composition classifier is expected to sit about 0.02 beyond the null ({T:poscon}), so we read this as a calibration check. The offsets were heterogeneous (Cochran's Q p = 0.018); pooled by random effects they were −0.021 (90% CI −0.060 to 0.017) for specificity and −0.009 (−0.039 to 0.021) for strength matching ({T:spikev12}), equivalent to zero within ±0.05 for strength matching only.

The coupling came from the direction the classifier gives each cell-type class ({T:direction}). Giving every gene the mean centroid row of its specificity class reproduced it (ρ −0.77 to −0.96), whereas random signs removed it (median ρ −0.03 to 0.03).

The spike-in showed what the benchmark could detect (Fig. 3d; {T:spike}). The upper-tail test reached 80% power for a composition-independent component of about 5% of C−A variance in GSE125583, but only of 21% in GSE33000, 29% in GSE132903 and 52% in GSE118553 (50% power at 3%, 16%, 23% and 34%). Assuming no offset from the null, one-sided 95% upper limits were 3% (GSE132903), 6% (GSE125583) and 21% (GSE118553).

### Severity normalisation keeps the neuron axis; cell-type normalisation shows unstable labels

In each MSBB region our approximation of CDR normalisation removed the neuron–CDR association (median ρ −0.22 to −0.01) but left the C−A score coupled to the neuron score (median ρ −0.91; Fig. 3c), as expected from the provenance of the centroids and from Neff et al., who reported fewer neurons in class C (their Figs 4A and 5D).

BRETIGEA SPVs explained a median 86% of C−A variance in MSBB regions and 82–92% in the GEO cohorts, but six random components already explained 76% and 50–84% ({T:bretigea}); the SPVs explained more in 9 of 17 regions and three of four GEO cohorts. After normalisation the score no longer tracked the neuron score (median ρ 0.01) and most patients changed class (median κ 0.15 in MSBB; 0.10–0.26 in GEO). Random components also changed most labels (median κ 0.29; 0.19–0.53), although in the parahippocampal gyrus, the discovery region, the SPVs changed fewer labels than random components did (κ 0.36 against 0.29); there the CDR association fell (0.55 to 0.16) and the tangle association rose (0.31 to 0.43). A single neuronal SPV changed labels as much as random 14-gene components ({T:spv}). Cell-type normalisation thus removes the neuronal part of the axis, but labels are unstable under any adjustment that removes the leading axes of the data.

### Case–control separation and stage follow the neuronal signal

The C−A score separated AD from controls (age- and sex-adjusted pooled ρ = 0.43, 95% CI 0.34–0.51; I² = 0; Fig. 4c), as did the neuron score (−0.45). After neuron adjustment the partial correlation was 0.14 (0.04–0.23), and 0.06–0.24 under other models of neuronal signal ({T:stageextra}; {T:neuronadj}); the reverse partial was −0.15. The remainder was not larger than with expression-matched random genes in any cohort ({T:adjnull}).

Within AD the C−A score rose with Braak stage in GSE125583 (ρ = 0.26, 0.12–0.39; Fig. 4a) and MSBB (0.32, 0.08–0.54) but not with CDR (0.18, −0.09 to 0.42; Fig. 4b), consistent with Neff et al.'s non-significant Kruskal–Wallis tests (CDR p = 0.082 in all cases; CDR and Braak p = 0.155 and 0.075 in 55 cases). Across 106 MSBB subjects it rose with tangle density (0.26, 0.08–0.44) but not with neuritic plaque density (0.11, −0.08 to 0.29) ({T:nft}), which does not reproduce the amyloid predominance Neff et al. reported for class C. After neuron adjustment Braak and tangle associations were near zero (GSE125583 Braak 0.00, −0.14 to 0.14; MSBB tangles 0.02, −0.17 to 0.21; {T:stagefu}), and forward and reverse models fitted equally well ({T:mediation}).

### Cross-region agreement, single-nucleus data and the C subtypes

A subject's C−A scores agreed across MSBB regions (ICC 0.61, 0.54–0.68) as much as those of specificity-matched random genes (0.60, p = 0.33), and subject means of the C−A and neuron scores correlated at −0.96 ({T:crossregion}).

Bulk data cannot separate fewer neurons from less RNA per neuron; in simulation neuron loss alone reproduced all three classes (Fig. 5b). In 84 SEA-AD donors (exploratory) the all-nuclei score rose with pseudo-progression (ρ = 0.25), Braak stage (0.39) and dementia (0.38) (Fig. 5a; {T:seaadpb}). Within single cell types it rose modestly with Braak stage in inhibitory pseudobulks (0.29), and lower NeuN density went with a class C shift in glial but not neuronal pseudobulks ({T:histo}), so the axis includes changes of cell state as well as composition. Within neuronal nuclei, Neff et al.'s regulators ATP6V1A and NAPB were lower in donors with higher scores, as reported, whereas SYT1 was higher ({T:regloo}; {T:regulators}).

The class B score and the B1−B2 contrast tracked cell scores about as matched random genes did ({T:cohortv11}). The C1−C2 contrast was the clear exception: its coupling with the neuron score (0.34–0.52 in the arrays, −0.04 in GSE125583) differed in all four cohorts from what the classifier's cell-type directions predict (within-class permutation medians −0.46 to −0.75; p ≤ 0.01; {T:direction}). The split of class C is therefore the part least explained by composition.

### What remains after composition adjustment

Cell scores explained 82–89% of the C−A variance ({T:residual}). The remainder contained a composition-adjusted axis that replicated across cohorts and in GTEx donors without dementia and resembled the signature of RNA integrity (r = 0.43–0.50; {T:hallmark}; Supplementary Note S8.5). The residual of the Neff genome-wide signatures after cell-type prediction still gave scores coupled to the neuron score (ρ −0.82 to −0.90; {T:signature}).

## Discussion

The main axes of the Neff classifier, two of three Li subgroups, most Lai and Ma pairs and de novo subtypes in three of four cohorts followed the neuronal signal of the sample; in the fourth, de novo subtypes followed vascular and microglial signal. For the Neff classifier, random genes with its cell-type mix reproduced the coupling through the direction it gives each cell-type class. Within AD in bulk cortex, the largest axis of most gene selections and of the whole transcriptome is composition, so clustering splits samples along it, and the genes that separate the clusters are then named by pathways that are themselves cell-type programmes. Eteleeb et al. (2024) and Al-Ouran et al. (2026) found neuronal loss and sampling location among the factors of large AMP-AD cohorts. What our results add is a benchmark that asks whether a subtype axis carries anything beyond composition, with a spike-in that states its power. That power was good in one RNA-seq cohort and modest in arrays, so a composition-independent component is excluded only down to about 5% of the Neff score in RNA-seq and a fifth or more in arrays.

### Relation to the original study

Our results speak to one claim of Neff et al., not to their subtypes as a whole. They reported the neuronal difference between classes, limited their independence claim to change "seen in later stages of AD" and anticipated contributions of neuronal and glial subpopulations. Severity normalisation removes neuronal variation that tracks dementia severity, but differences between patients at similar severity remain and the main axis captures them. Neff et al. kept severity normalisation because further SPV normalisation did not reduce the remaining correlation in MSBB (their fig. S2C) and left residual correlation with cognition in ROSMAP. In our reproduction cell-type normalisation reassigned most patients, but so did random components, so the class structure is not specifically what it removes. Whether neuronal differences between patients are a confound or a meaningful dimension, such as vulnerability or resilience, our data do not settle.

Some results point the other way. In GSE125583, the one cohort with power for a component of about 5%, the classifier was slightly less coupled than matched genes and kept a neuron-adjusted case–control remainder; the C1−C2 split did not follow composition; and in the parahippocampal gyrus the tangle association rose after cell-type normalisation. Our cohorts were mostly prefrontal and temporal arrays rather than parahippocampal RNA-seq, so effect sizes in the original setting may differ.

### What the axis measures

The neuron score reflects neuronal loss, loss of synaptic transcripts per neuron, which precedes neuron loss (DeKosky and Scheff, 1990; Terry et al., 1991), dissection, and pre-analytical factors (Li et al., 2004); the SEA-AD data point to cell state as well as number. The axis is best described as the local neurodegeneration signal of the sampled tissue. It tracked tangles more than plaques, as neuronal loss does (Gómez-Isla et al., 1997), which fits Neff et al.'s regional tangle differences but not their description of class C as amyloid-predominant. This does not make it uninteresting: the best-validated AD subtypes are defined by where tangles and neuronal loss fall (Murray et al., 2011), and a class C–class A split in parahippocampal tissue could be a trace of limbic-predominant against hippocampal-sparing disease. Testing this needs regional tangle counts, which the public MSBB deposit lacks. Neuron adjustment reduced stage associations to small remainders, but because tangles may cause neuronal loss and the reverse model fitted equally well, the data show neither that the subtypes are stages nor that they are independent of stage.

### Implications and recommendations

Subtype names drawn from pathway enrichment should be read against the cell-type programmes those pathways belong to, and regulators nominated from bulk subtypes should be checked within cell types; ATP6V1A and NAPB passed that check, SYT1 did not. We suggest that subtype studies of bulk brain (1) compare subtype signal with random genes matched on expression and cell-type specificity, in both directions, and randomise its cell-type directions; (2) add a composition-independent signal to the real data and report the power of that comparison; (3) report subtype scores as continuous variables with and without composition adjustment by two methods; and (4) report RNA quality, dissection, local neuropathology and co-pathology.

### Limitations

The subtypes were approximated by centroid projection, mostly on arrays and outside the discovery region, without the original RNA-seq data, labels or classifier; the decisive test would relate bulk RNA-seq to same-tissue cell counts, for example in ROSMAP (Patrick et al., 2020). The other schemes were approximated by scores or principal components of their published genes, Lai by its diagnostic signature. The selection rule was stated after the Neff analysis, and the large AMP-AD schemes were represented only by de novo clustering, whose random-gene comparison has little power. Power assumes a signal along the published contrast with nulls computed on the original samples; upper limits also assume no offset, and equivalence margins were chosen post hoc. Cell-type-matched nulls cannot exclude biology aligned with cell-type programmes. MSBB regions had few AD subjects for ten covariates, RNA quality was unavailable for the GEO cohorts, co-pathology was available only in SEA-AD, AD definitions differed between cohorts, and endpoints were fixed after exploration. Further limitations are listed in Supplementary Note S9.

## Conclusions

In bulk AD cortex, the main axis of the Neff et al. (2021) subtypes, of several published gene-set schemes and of de novo subtypes is largely cell composition, mostly neuronal signal. For the Neff classifier, random genes with the same cell-type mix recover this axis; the split of class C is the exception. A composition-independent component could be excluded down to about 5% of score variance only in RNA-seq. Subtypes of bulk brain tissue should be tested against cell-type-matched random genes, with a spike-in that reports the power of the comparison, before they are interpreted as distinct mechanisms or used for target nomination and stratification.
